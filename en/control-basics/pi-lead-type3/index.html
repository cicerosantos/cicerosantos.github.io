Control basics

PI, phase lead and the type III

The integrator brings the steady-state error to zero and costs 90° of phase; phase lead gives phase back at the crossover; the type III is an integrator with two leads, and the 2p2z is its digital relative.

First, see: Feedback and margins, First-order pole and zero

A compensator is made of the same first-order blocks as the plant, chosen to give the loop gain two things: high gain at low frequency and enough phase at the crossover.

The integrator: zero steady-state error

With the loop closed, the steady-state error to a reference step is 1/(1+T(0))1/(1 + T(0))1/(1+T(0)). If the compensator is just a gain KKK, T(0)=K Gvd(0)T(0) = K\,G_{vd}(0)T(0)=KGvd​(0) is finite, and some error always remains. In the buck, Gvd(0)=23.9G_{vd}(0) = 23.9Gvd​(0)=23.9: for less than 1 % error, KKK would have to exceed 4, and the crossover would go where the phase leaves no margin.

A pole at the origin, KI/sK_I/sKI​/s, makes T(0)T(0)T(0) infinite, and the error to a step goes to zero. The price is the integrator's phase, −90° at every frequency, which adds to the plant's.

The PI: integrator plus a zero

Gc(s)=KI 1+s/ωzsG_c(s) = K_I\,\frac{1 + s/\omega_z}{s}Gc​(s)=KI​s1+s/ωz​​

The zero gives phase back above ωz\omega_zωz​: well above it, the PI behaves like a gain, with phase near 0°. With the zero a decade below the crossover, the integrator barely weighs on the margin. But the PI never gives positive phase, and the buck, above resonance, is already near −180°.

The type III: integrator plus two leads

The analog type III compensator of a voltage-mode buck is an integrator with two leads, usually equal:

Gc(s)=KIs(1+s/ω01+s/ωp)2,KI=K ω0G_c(s) = \frac{K_I}{s}\left(\frac{1 + s/\omega_0}{1 + s/\omega_p}\right)^2, \qquad K_I = K\,\omega_0Gc​(s)=sKI​​(1+s/ωp​1+s/ω0​​)2,KI​=Kω0​

The two zeros sit near the LC resonance, where the plant takes up to 180°, and give up to 180° of phase, minus what the poles take; the two poles limit the gain above the crossover.

In the converters

The buck's 2p2z has an integrator, two zeros at 2 kHz and one pole at 60 kHz: one high-frequency pole fewer than the type III. It reads as a PI times a lead:

Exercise

Predict, then check in the figure. Move the 2p2z's zero 2 from 2 kHz to 8 kHz, onto the crossover. How much phase does that zero stop giving, and what should the new margin be?

Answer

At 8 kHz, the zero at 2 kHz gave arctan⁡4=76∘\arctan 4 = 76^\circarctan4=76∘; at 8 kHz, it gives arctan⁡1=45∘\arctan 1 = 45^\circarctan1=45∘. 31° are lost, and the margin falls from 41° to about 10°. The figure's model gives 10.6°.

Exercise

A colleague swaps the 2p2z for a PI with its zero at 2 kHz, keeps the crossover at 8 kHz, and the buck starts oscillating. Explain it with the phase.

Answer

The PI has negative phase at every frequency: −14° at 8 kHz. Added to the plant's −191° with the delay, that makes −205°: the phase passed −180° before the crossover, and the margin is negative. In the lab's buck, with the ESR zero at 677 kHz, far above the crossover, a PI only works with the crossover below the resonance, where the plant has not yet taken phase; above it, lead is needed. It is not a general rule: with an ESR zero near the crossover, as with an electrolytic capacitor, that zero gives phase back, and a type II (PI plus a pole) can cross above the resonance.
