Right-half-plane pole and zero

The pole 1/(1−s/ωp)1/(1 - s/\omega_p)1/(1−s/ωp​) and the zero 1−s/ωz1 - s/\omega_z1−s/ωz​ have the magnitude of the ordinary forms and the mirrored phase, from 0° to +90° and from 0° to −90°; only the phase tells the two sides of the s-plane apart.

First, see: First-order pole and zero

In First-order pole and zero, the pole and the zero sit at s=−ωs = -\omegas=−ω, in the left half-plane. Change the sign of the term in sss and the root moves to s=+ωs = +\omegas=+ω, the right half-plane (RHP):

The magnitude does not see the side

At s=jωs = j\omegas=jω, the RHP zero is 1−j f/fz1 - j\,f/f_z1−jf/fz​. Its magnitude, 1+(f/fz)2\sqrt{1 + (f/f_z)^2}1+(f/fz​)2​, is that of the ordinary zero, 1+j f/fz1 + j\,f/f_z1+jf/fz​: the two are conjugates. The magnitude asymptote is identical: 0 dB up to fzf_zfz​, then +20 dB per decade, with a 3 dB error at the corner. The same holds for the RHP pole, which has the magnitude of the ordinary pole.

The phase does

The phase lines are the usual ones, from one decade below the corner to one decade above, ±45° per decade, and they pass through ±45° at the corner. As in the ordinary forms, each factor tends to 1 at DC, and the phase starts from 0° there.

The RHP zero has a zero's magnitude and a pole's phase; the RHP pole, a pole's magnitude and a zero's phase. The magnitude alone does not show the side of the root: only the phase reveals the half-plane. When reading a Bode plot, check both: a magnitude rising 20 dB per decade while the phase falls is an RHP zero.

A function with a zero in the right half-plane is non-minimum phase: among the functions with the same magnitude, it does not have the smallest phase change. The minimum-phase one is that with the zero mirrored to the left.

In time

The side also changes the response. An RHP pole gives a term e+t/τe^{+t/\tau}e+t/τ, which grows without limit: the function is unstable. An RHP zero does not make the function unstable, because stability depends only on the poles (Transfer function and factored form), but it makes the step start in the direction opposite to the final value.

Two associations, which the next units explore:

  • the boost has a right-half-plane zero in its plant, and it limits the crossover: Right-half-plane zero: the effect on the loop;
  • a plant with a right-half-plane pole is unstable in open loop, and only the loop stabilizes it. An example: a regulated converter draws constant power, and for small signals its input is a negative resistance; with an LC filter in front, the combination can have poles in the right half-plane.

In the converters

In the lab's boost, at 12 V input and 2 A load, the plant has the factor 1−s/ωz1 - s/\omega_z1−s/ωz​ with fz=6.9f_z = 6.9fz​=6.9 kHz (The right-half-plane zero). In magnitude, it equals an ordinary zero at 6.9 kHz; in phase, it takes 12.3° at 1.5 kHz and 45° at 6.9 kHz, where an ordinary zero would give the same value, but positive.
