Inverted forms and the sign on the Bode plot

A factor written in ω/s\omega/sω/s is 1 at high frequency and is an integrator or a differentiator times the ordinary form, sometimes with a minus sign; with that decomposition and the ±180° rule, factor by factor, the four inverted forms are drawn by asymptotes.

First, see: Pole and zero at the origin: integrator and differentiator, Right-half-plane pole and zero

The factored form writes each factor as 1+s/ω1 + s/\omega1+s/ω, which is 1 at DC. The same factor can be written the other way round, in ω/s\omega/sω/s:

inverted pole: 11+ωp/s,inverted zero: 1+ωzs,RHP: 11−ωp/s,1−ωzs\text{inverted pole: } \frac{1}{1 + \omega_p/s}, \quad \text{inverted zero: } 1 + \frac{\omega_z}{s}, \quad \text{RHP: } \frac{1}{1 - \omega_p/s}, \quad 1 - \frac{\omega_z}{s}inverted pole: 1+ωp​/s1​,inverted zero: 1+sωz​​,RHP: 1−ωp​/s1​,1−sωz​​

These are the inverted forms: the factor is 1 at high frequency, and the gain in front is the high-frequency gain. None needs to be memorized: they all reduce to the forms of the previous notes.

A differentiator or an integrator times the ordinary form

Factoring out ω/s\omega/sω/s:

11+ωp/s=s/ωp1+s/ωp,1+ωzs=ωzs(1+sωz)\frac{1}{1 + \omega_p/s} = \frac{s/\omega_p}{1 + s/\omega_p}, \qquad 1 + \frac{\omega_z}{s} = \frac{\omega_z}{s}\left(1 + \frac{s}{\omega_z}\right)1+ωp​/s1​=1+s/ωp​s/ωp​​,1+sωz​​=sωz​​(1+ωz​s​)

The inverted pole is a differentiator times an ordinary pole, with the same ω\omegaω: the differentiator's line rises +20 dB per decade and crosses 0 dB at fpf_pfp​, exactly where the pole starts falling −20 dB per decade. Added, the two lines give +20 dB per decade up to fpf_pfp​ and 0 dB after. In phase, the differentiator gives +90°, and the pole, from 0° to −90°: the sum goes from +90° to 0°. The inverted zero is the mirror: an integrator times an ordinary zero, −20 dB per decade up to fzf_zfz​, and the phase from −90° to 0°. That is why the inverted pole brings a zero at the origin, and the inverted zero, a pole at the origin (Pole and zero at the origin: integrator and differentiator).

With the root in the right half-plane, a minus sign appears:

The minus sign: ±180°, factor by factor

The phase rule is that of First-order pole and zero: each factor has 0° of phase at the end where it tends to 1, and its phase is continuous from there. In the inverted forms, that end is high frequency. A minus sign adds +180° or −180°, which give the same complex number; choose, for each factor, the one that brings its phase to 0° at high frequency.

In the inverted RHP zero: the integrator gives −90°; the RHP zero, from 0° to −90°. At high frequency the sum is −180°, and the sign enters as +180°: the phase goes from −90∘+0∘+180∘=+90∘-90^\circ + 0^\circ + 180^\circ = +90^\circ−90∘+0∘+180∘=+90∘, below fz/10f_z/10fz​/10, to 0°, above 10fz10 f_z10fz​. In the inverted RHP pole, the differentiator gives +90° and the RHP pole, from 0° to +90°: the sign enters as −180°, and the phase goes from −90° to 0°.

To check, evaluate the dominant term at low frequency: 1−ωz/s≈−ωz/(jω)=+j ωz/ω1 - \omega_z/s \approx -\omega_z/(j\omega) = +j\,\omega_z/\omega1−ωz​/s≈−ωz​/(jω)=+jωz​/ω, a positive imaginary number, +90°.

In the whole function, the total phase is the sum of the factors' phases, and a negative gain adds +180°. A calculation tool may show the same curve shifted by 360°: the complex number is the same.

By hand, in four steps

  1. Factor out ω/s\omega/sω/s in each inverted factor.
  2. Replace each term with an integrator, a differentiator, a pole or a zero, on either side.
  3. Add the magnitude and phase lines.
  4. Correct each minus sign with ±180°, by the end where the factor tends to 1, and check with the dominant term.

In the converters

In the buck's 2p2z, the integrator and the first zero, at 2 kHz, form an inverted zero, 1+ωz/s1 + \omega_z/s1+ωz​/s: below 2 kHz the magnitude falls 20 dB per decade; above, the factor tends to 1, and the phase rises toward 0°. At 8 kHz it is −arctan⁡(2/8)=−14.0∘-\arctan(2/8) = -14.0^\circ−arctan(2/8)=−14.0∘. It is the form in which the PI and the lag compensator are usually written (PI, phase lead and the type III, The phase-lag compensator).
