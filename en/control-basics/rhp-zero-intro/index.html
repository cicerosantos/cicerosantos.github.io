Right-half-plane zero

A zero right of the jω axis has an ordinary zero's magnitude and a pole's phase; it destabilises nothing, but makes the step start the wrong way and limits the crossover.

A pole's phase, a zero's magnitude

An ordinary pole and a right-half-plane zero both take 90° of phase. The difference is in the magnitude: the pole brings the gain down, the right-half-plane zero raises it. For the loop it is the worst combination: more gain and less phase at the same time.

It is not an unstable pole

Stability depends only on the poles. A system with all its poles on the left and a zero on the right is stable: its step response settles. It is non-minimum phase: it has more phase lag than the function with the same magnitude and the zero mirrored to the left, the minimum-phase one.

What the zero changes in time is the start. Right after the step, the output moves the opposite way to its final value, and only then turns around: this is the inverse initial response.

Why it limits the crossover

A compensator cancels an ordinary zero by putting a pole on top of it. To cancel a right-half-plane zero it would need a right-half-plane pole: an unstable controller. That is not done. What is left is to cross well below the zero, where its lag arctan⁡(fc/fz)\arctan(f_c/f_z)arctan(fc​/fz​) is still small and phase margin is left. Look at the dip: in the lab, the zero sits 8 to 20 times above the resonance, which lies between 570 and 690 Hz, and the plant's inverse response barely shows in the step, a fraction of 1 % for a few tens of microseconds. The zero's harm is not in the dip; it is in the phase it takes near the crossover.

The controls go a little beyond what the lab simulates. At the worst end, 9 V and 24 Ω, the inductor current reaches 11 A, the zero falls to 3.7 kHz and the resonance to 511 Hz: the zero already takes 22° at 1.5 kHz, but the dip is still only 0.9 %. At the other end, 15 V and 480 Ω, the zero rises to 226 kHz, beyond the ESR zero, and takes almost no phase at the crossover.

Exercise

Predict, then check in the figure. In the first figure, the zero starts at +2 kHz, in the right half-plane. Before pressing the button that mirrors it to −2 kHz: what happens to the magnitude, to the phase at 2 kHz and to the start of the step?

Answer

The magnitude does not change. The zero's contribution at 2 kHz goes from −45° to +45°: the phase curve rises 90° there, and 180° at high frequency. The step, which used to start downward, now rises straight to the same final value, because the DC gain did not change.

Exercise

A boost rings after a 1 to 2 A load step. A colleague proposes raising the compensator's gain to "make it stiffer". What do you answer?

Answer

That it will get worse. At 2 A the zero drops to 6.9 kHz. More gain moves the crossover up, to where the zero takes more phase (30° at 4 kHz against 12° at 1.5 kHz), and the margin vanishes. The right move is to lower the crossover, or to design for the worst case from the start: maximum load with minimum input.
