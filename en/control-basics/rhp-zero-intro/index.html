Right-half-plane zero

A zero right of the jω axis has an ordinary zero's magnitude and a pole's phase; it takes phase away from the loop, makes the step start the wrong way, and limits the crossover.

First, see: First-order pole and zero, Feedback and margins

The magnitude does not see the side; the phase does

Pole's phase, zero's magnitude

An ordinary pole and a right-half-plane zero both take 90° of phase. The difference is in the magnitude: the pole lowers the gain, the right-half-plane zero raises it. For the loop, that is the worst combination: more gain and less phase at once.

It is not an unstable pole

In open loop, stability depends only on the plant's poles. A system with all poles on the left and a zero on the right is stable: its step response settles. It is non-minimum phase: it has more phase lag than the function of the same magnitude with the zero mirrored to the left, the minimum-phase one.

What the zero changes in time is the start. Right after the step, the output moves in the opposite direction to its final value, and only then turns: this is the initial inverse response.

Why it limits the crossover

In closed loop, the zero acts through phase. The loop closes when TTT reaches −1 (Feedback and margins), and the zero takes away phase right at fcf_cfc​: the lag is arctan⁡(fc/fRHP)\arctan(f_c/f_{\mathrm{RHP}})arctan(fc​/fRHP​), which grows with the crossover. More gain raises the crossover, which takes away more phase, and the margin vanishes. In the Root locus (qualitative reading), the zero pulls a branch into the right half-plane.

A compensator cannot cancel this zero. Canceling a right-half-plane zero would need a right-half-plane pole, that is, an unstable controller (Pole-zero cancellation and its risks). What remains is to cross below it. At the validated point of the lab, 12 V in and 2 A of load (24 Ω), the zero sits at 6.9 kHz. The 2p2z crosses at 1.5 kHz, where the zero takes away 12.3°; at 4 kHz, it would take 30.2°. The 1.5 kHz crossover is fRHP/4.6f_{\mathrm{RHP}}/4.6fRHP​/4.6, between the 1/5 and 1/3 rules. At that point, the 1/3 window is open, from 2060 to 2290 Hz. At 10 V and 2 A, the worst corner, it is empty.

The inverse response exists, but what weighs on the design is the phase near the crossover, not the start of the step.

Exercise

Predict, then check on the figure. In the first figure, the zero starts at +2 kHz, in the right half-plane. Before pressing the button that mirrors it to −2 kHz: what happens to the magnitude, to the phase at 2 kHz and to the start of the step?

Answer

The magnitude does not change. The zero's contribution at 2 kHz goes from −45° to +45°: the phase curve rises 90° there, and 180° at high frequency. The step, which used to start falling, now rises straight to the same final value, because the DC gain has not changed.

Exercise

A boost rings after a load step from 1 to 2 A. A colleague proposes raising the compensator's gain to make it "firmer". What do you answer?

Answer

That it will get worse. At 2 A, the zero is at 6.9 kHz. More gain pushes the crossover up, where the zero takes away more phase (30° at 4 kHz, against 12° at 1.5 kHz), and the margin shrinks. The right move is to lower the crossover, or to design for the worst corner, which in the lab is 10 V and 2 A.
