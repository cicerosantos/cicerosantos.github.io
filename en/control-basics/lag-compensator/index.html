The phase-lag compensator

A pole followed by a higher zero, well below the crossover, multiplies the low-frequency gain by the factor α and costs a few degrees of margin; with no pole at the origin, it reduces the step error but does not zero it.

First, see: Steady-state error and system type, PI, phase lead and the type III

Phase lead solves the margin: it puts phase at the crossover. The steady-state error is another problem: it asks for gain at low frequency, far from the crossover. The integrator gives infinite gain there, but costs −90° and accumulates the error when the duty cycle saturates (Anti-windup and duty saturation). The phase-lag compensator gives finite gain, multiplied by a factor, and leaves the crossover nearly where it was.

The form

Gc(s)=α 1+s/ωz1+s/ωp,α=ωzωp>1G_c(s) = \alpha\,\frac{1 + s/\omega_z}{1 + s/\omega_p}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{\omega_z}{\omega_p} > 1Gc​(s)=α1+s/ωp​1+s/ωz​​,α=ωp​ωz​​>1

It is phase lead with the zero and the pole swapped: the same ratio α\alphaα, now with the pole below. At low frequency, the gain is α\alphaα. Between the pole and the zero, the magnitude falls 20 dB per decade. Above the zero, the gain is α ωp/ωz=1\alpha\,\omega_p/\omega_z = 1αωp​/ωz​=1. The same function, written in the inverted form, shows this directly:

Gc(s)=1+ωz/s1+ωp/sG_c(s) = \frac{1 + \omega_z/s}{1 + \omega_p/s}Gc​(s)=1+ωp​/s1+ωz​/s​

Taking ω/s\omega/sω/s out of each factor, the inverted zero's integrator and the inverted pole's differentiator cancel, and the ratio of the gains is left, ωz/ωp=α\omega_z/\omega_p = \alphaωz​/ωp​=α. The factor α\alphaα in front is what keeps the gain at 1 above the zero. Without it, the block would attenuate α\alphaα times at the crossover, and the crossover already designed would move down.

The phase is the mirror of lead's hill: a valley, with its bottom at the geometric mean fz fp\sqrt{f_z\,f_p}fz​fp​​ and the same formula, with the sign flipped:

sin⁡∣φmin⁡∣=α−1α+1\sin|\varphi_{\min}| = \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + 1}sin∣φmin​∣=α+1α−1​

With α=4\alpha = 4α=4, the valley reaches −36.9°; with α=9\alpha = 9α=9, −53.1°, the values in the lead table. This valley has to stay far from the crossover.

Where to put it: a decade below the crossover

At fcf_cfc​, the block's phase is

φ(fc)=arctan⁡fcfz−arctan⁡fcfp\varphi(f_c) = \arctan\frac{f_c}{f_z} - \arctan\frac{f_c}{f_p}φ(fc​)=arctanfz​fc​​−arctanfp​fc​​

With the zero a decade below, fz=fc/10f_z = f_c/10fz​=fc​/10, and α=4\alpha = 4α=4, it is arctan⁡10−arctan⁡40=84.3∘−88.6∘=−4.3∘\arctan 10 - \arctan 40 = 84.3^\circ - 88.6^\circ = -4.3^\circarctan10−arctan40=84.3∘−88.6∘=−4.3∘. With a larger α\alphaα, the second term approaches 90°, and the cost approaches 90∘−arctan⁡10=arctan⁡(1/10)=5.7∘90^\circ - \arctan 10 = \arctan(1/10) = 5.7^\circ90∘−arctan10=arctan(1/10)=5.7∘. It is a ceiling: with the zero a decade below, the block never takes more than 5.7° at the crossover, whatever α\alphaα. The magnitude there is +0.04 dB, and the crossover barely moves.

The recipe has four steps:

  1. α\alphaα is the ratio between the KpK_pKp​ required and the one the loop already has.
  2. The zero goes a decade below the crossover, fz=fc/10f_z = f_c/10fz​=fc​/10.
  3. The pole goes at fp=fz/αf_p = f_z/\alphafp​=fz​/α.
  4. Check the margin. If the cost is more than acceptable, move the pair down.

"A decade" is the minimum. With the zero four times below fcf_cfc​ and α=4\alpha = 4α=4, the cost is already arctan⁡4−arctan⁡16=−10.5∘\arctan 4 - \arctan 16 = -10.5^\circarctan4−arctan16=−10.5∘. The block also leaves a tail. It creates a closed-loop pole between its pole and its zero. That pole sits near the zero only when the loop gain there is high. Part of the error fades slowly, with a time constant of the order of 1/(2πfz)1/(2\pi f_z)1/(2πfz​). In the buck below, with Kp=0.4K_p = 0.4Kp​=0.4, the pole is at −351 Hz, with τ=0.45\tau = 0.45τ=0.45 ms, and carries 30 % of the step. Moving the pair down lengthens the tail: with the zero at 80 Hz, τ=4.3\tau = 4.3τ=4.3 ms.

It does not zero the error

The block's pole is not at the origin, and the loop's type does not change. On a type 0 plant, the block multiplies KpK_pKp​ by α\alphaα, and the step error goes from 1/(1+Kp)1/(1 + K_p)1/(1+Kp​) to 1/(1+αKp)1/(1 + \alpha K_p)1/(1+αKp​). It shrinks, but does not go to zero. And it shrinks α\alphaα times only when KpK_pKp​ is large: the ratio is (1+αKp)/(1+Kp)(1 + \alpha K_p)/(1 + K_p)(1+αKp​)/(1+Kp​), which tends to α\alphaα with Kp≫1K_p \gg 1Kp​≫1 and stays well below it with KpK_pKp​ near 1.

The PI is the block's limit: with the pole taken to the origin, fp→0f_p \to 0fp​→0, the factor α\alphaα goes to infinity, and the block becomes an integrator with a zero.

With the phase lag, α=4\alpha = 4α=4, zero at 800 Hz and pole at 200 Hz, the crossover goes to 8.02 kHz and the margin falls 4.3°, to 53.0°. KpK_pKp​ becomes 1.58, and the error 1/2.58=391/2.58 = 391/2.58=39 %: 1.85 times smaller, not 4, because KpK_pKp​ was below 1. Exercise

A phase lead and a phase lag both have α=9\alpha = 9α=9. What is the peak of one and the valley of the other? Where does each go, relative to the crossover?

Answer

sin⁡φ=(9−1)/(9+1)=0.8\sin\varphi = (9 - 1)/(9 + 1) = 0.8sinφ=(9−1)/(9+1)=0.8, and φ=53.1∘\varphi = 53.1^\circφ=53.1∘: +53.1° at the lead's peak, −53.1° at the lag's valley, both at the geometric mean of the zero and the pole. The lead's peak goes on the crossover, where phase is missing; the lag's valley goes at least a decade below, where phase is to spare.
