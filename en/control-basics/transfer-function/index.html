Transfer function and factored form

The transfer function is a ratio of polynomials. Written in factored form, the DC gain stands alone in front, and each pole and zero marks a frequency on the Bode plot.

This note opens the unit Read the plant. It teaches one skill: write a converter's plant in factored form, and recognize in each factor a frequency of the Bode plot. The starting equation is the buck's plant (The buck's transfer function Gvd(s)G_{vd}(s)Gvd​(s)):

It has three parts: a gain, a zero and a pair of poles. This note teaches you to name them.

A ratio of polynomials

In a linear, time-invariant system at rest at time zero, the ratio between the transform of the output and that of the input is a fixed function of the system:

G(s)=Y(s)U(s)=N(s)D(s)G(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \frac{N(s)}{D(s)}G(s)=U(s)Y(s)​=D(s)N(s)​

In the buck, the input is the small variation of the duty cycle, d^\hat dd^, and the output is the small variation of the output voltage, v^o\hat v_ov^o​, around the operating point (The averaged switch model). The polynomials have real coefficients, and their roots have names:

  • zeros are the roots of N(s)N(s)N(s): there, G(s)=0G(s) = 0G(s)=0;
  • poles are the roots of D(s)D(s)D(s): there, G(s)G(s)G(s) goes to infinity;
  • the order is the degree of D(s)D(s)D(s). The buck is second order, because it stores energy in two elements, the inductor and the capacitor. In a circuit, the order does not exceed the number of these elements.

Since the degree of N(s)N(s)N(s) does not exceed that of D(s)D(s)D(s), the function is proper: the gain does not grow without limit at high frequency. Circuits built from resistors, inductors and capacitors always give proper functions.

A root in the right half-plane enters with its sign changed. A zero in the right half-plane has the same magnitude as a zero in the left half-plane: only the phase differs (Right-half-plane zero).

Pole or zero frequency

The root at s=−ωs = -\omegas=−ω corresponds to the frequency ω\omegaω, in rad/s, or f=ω/2πf = \omega/2\pif=ω/2π, in Hz: the pole or zero frequency. The figures in this course mark the s-plane axes in hertz, s/2πs/2\pis/2π.

The s-plane

Each root is a point in the complex plane. The real part says how the response decays: the further left of the jωj\omegajω axis, the faster. The imaginary part says whether the response oscillates, and at what frequency. A pole in the right half-plane would make the response grow without limit. In open loop, therefore, stability depends only on the poles. A zero in the right half-plane does not make the plant unstable, but it has an effect on the loop, which the note on the right-half-plane zero explains.
