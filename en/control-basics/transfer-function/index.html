Transfer function, poles and zeros

A transfer function is a ratio of two polynomials in s; the DC gain, the poles and the zeros, written in standard form, tell almost everything about the Bode plot and the step.

If it looks like a closed box, start here. It has three pieces, and each has a name: a gain, a zero and a pair of poles. This note teaches you to recognise them.

A ratio of polynomials

In a linear time-invariant system, at rest at time zero, the Laplace transforms of the output and of the input keep a fixed ratio that depends only on the system:

G(s)=Y(s)U(s)=N(s)D(s)G(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)} = \frac{N(s)}{D(s)}G(s)=U(s)Y(s)​=D(s)N(s)​

In the buck, the input is the small change of the duty, d^\hat dd^, and the output is the small change of the output voltage, v^o\hat v_ov^o​, around the operating point (The averaged switch model). Both polynomials have real coefficients, and their roots have names:

  • zeros are the roots of the numerator N(s)N(s)N(s): there, G(s)=0G(s) = 0G(s)=0;
  • poles are the roots of the denominator D(s)D(s)D(s): there, G(s)G(s)G(s) goes to infinity;
  • the order is the degree of D(s)D(s)D(s). The buck is second order: it stores energy in two places, the inductor and the capacitor. In a passive circuit like this one, each pole points to an element that stores energy; the compensator and the firmware's delay do not follow that rule.

A function whose numerator degree is less than or equal to the denominator's is proper, and only those exist in a circuit: the gain does not grow without bound at high frequency.

Pole and zero frequency

The root at s=−ωs = -\omegas=−ω corresponds to the frequency ω\omegaω, in rad/s, or f=ω/2πf = \omega/2\pif=ω/2π, in Hz: the pole frequency or zero frequency. This course's figures mark the s-plane's axes in hertz, s/2πs/2\pis/2π.

The s-plane

Each root is a point in the complex plane. The real part says how the response decays: left of the jωj\omegajω axis it decays, and the further left, the faster. The imaginary part says whether it oscillates, and at what frequency. A pole right of the axis makes the response grow without bound. A zero on the right destabilises nothing, but has effects of its own (Right-half-plane zero).
