Control basics

Start here

What the units of classical control are for, and ten questions on the first three, reading the plant, closing the loop and compensating, to find out, in a few minutes, which of their notes you need to read.

How numbers are written here

In this material, the decimal mark is the point, as in 9.0 V, 1.5 kHz and 0.6 dB, throughout the text, the equations and the figures. The comma only separates the items of a list. So the material matches what one reads in other sources and on instruments.

The converter and digital-control notes use, without stopping to explain them, poles and zeros, the factored form, the Bode plot and the QQQ factor. This section holds four units of classical control, and the questions cover the first three: reading the plant, writing it in factored form and drawing its Bode plot with asymptotes; closing the loop, with the margins; and compensating, with the steady-state error and the compensator that deals with it. The fourth, the root locus, is optional. In unit 1, the forms and the exercises are generic, and the converters come in as examples; in the others, the examples come from the lab's converters, and each note ends with the way back to the point that asked for it.

You do not need to read the whole section. Answer the questions below in your head, with paper and a calculator if you like, and only then open the answer. If you get one wrong, the answer says which note to read. If you get them all right, go on to the converter trails. Unit 2: closing the loop

7. Phase margin

The buck's loop crosses at 8 kHz with 41° of phase margin. How much more delay does it tolerate before oscillating?

Answer

41∘/(360∘×8 kHz)=14.241^\circ/(360^\circ \times 8\,\text{kHz}) = 14.241∘/(360∘×8kHz)=14.2 µs, almost three switching periods (2.8). If you got it wrong, read Feedback and margins.

8. The step that starts backwards

In the boost, a step in the duty cycle makes the output first fall a little and only then rise. What singularity explains this? Does it make the boost unstable?

Answer

A right-half-plane zero. No: open-loop stability depends only on the poles. The zero takes phase like a pole and limits the crossover frequency. If you got it wrong, read Right-half-plane zero.
