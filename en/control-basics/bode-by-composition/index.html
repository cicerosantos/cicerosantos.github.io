Bode by composition: from factored form to asymptote

Two tables sum up the first- and second-order forms, and the recipe works both ways, adding up the factors of G(s)G(s)G(s) into the asymptotic Bode plot and reading G(s)G(s)G(s) back from it.

In a factored function, the factors' magnitudes add up in dB, and their phases in degrees: the tables sum up the unit's forms; the recipe adds them both ways.

The phase rule

Each factor has a phase of 0° at the end where it tends to 1 (DC in the ordinary and RHP forms, high frequency in the inverted ones). The total phase is the sum of the factors' phases, plus 180° if K<0K < 0K<0; a program may show it shifted by 360°.

The recipe

From the equation to the plot.

  1. Write G(s)G(s)G(s) as KKK times table factors, naming each form.
  2. List the corners in increasing order.
  3. At low frequency, add the initial slopes (∓20 at the origin, ±20 or ±40 in the inverted forms, zero elsewhere) and find one point of the line. Where all factors are 1, the level is 20log⁡10∣K∣20\log_{10}|K|20log10​∣K∣. With a root at the origin, K(s/ωa)±1K(s/\omega_a)^{\pm 1}K(s/ωa​)±1 is 20log⁡10∣K∣20\log_{10}|K|20log10​∣K∣ dB at faf_afa​, even if faf_afa​ lies outside the range of the corners; carry that point to the first corner with ±20log⁡10(f/fa)\pm 20\log_{10}(f/f_a)±20log10​(f/fa​).
  4. At each corner, add the factor's change of slope: ±20 for first order, ±40 for a pair.
  5. Add up the phase lines, each from the table's initial value to its final one.
  6. Mark the error at the corner: 3 dB for a first-order factor, below for the pole and above for the zero; 20log⁡10Q20\log_{10} Q20log10​Q dB for a pair, above for poles and below for zeros (with Q<1Q < 1Q<1 the side flips).

Reading back: from the asymptote to the equation

  1. At low frequency: 0 dB per decade and 0°, a DC gain, at 20log⁡10∣K∣20\log_{10}|K|20log10​∣K∣; −20 and −90°, a pure integrator or an inverted zero; +20 and +90°, a pure differentiator or an inverted pole; −40 and −180°, a double pure integrator or an inverted zero pair; +40 and +180°, a double pure differentiator or an inverted pole pair.
  2. Each change of slope marks a corner: ±20 dB per decade, a first-order factor; ±40, a pair if there is a peak or a dip; a −40 with the curve 6 dB below is two equal real poles (Q=0.5Q = 0.5Q=0.5).
  3. If, at a corner, the magnitude and the phase move in opposite directions, the factor's root is in the right half-plane. Check by the final phase: with all roots on the left, it would be ±90° per first-order factor and ±180° per pair, plus that of the roots at the origin; each 180° less is an RHP zero, and each 360°, an RHP zero pair, even with overlapping ramps.
  4. A pair's QQQ comes from the real curve at f0f_0f0​: Q=10(curve−asymptote)/20Q = 10^{(\text{curve} - \text{asymptote})/20}Q=10(curve−asymptote)/20 for a pole pair and 10(asymptote−curve)/2010^{(\text{asymptote} - \text{curve})/20}10(asymptote−curve)/20 for a zero pair, if the other corners are more than a decade away. Pure integrators and differentiators do not disturb it: their asymptote is exact.
