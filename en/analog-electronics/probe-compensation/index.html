Compensating the ×10 probe

The passive ×10 probe as two R∥C in series, with the resistive divider at DC and the capacitive one at an edge; R1C1 = R2C2 puts the zero on the pole, and a square wave shows whether the probe is overcompensated or undercompensated.

First, see: RC and RL circuits: from the circuit to the pole-zero form

A passive ×10 probe puts 9 MΩ in series with the oscilloscope's 1 MΩ input: the resistive divider gives 1/10. The input, however, has capacitance, and so does the cable. With some 200 pF and nothing else, the 900 kΩ of R1∥R2R_1 \parallel R_2R1​∥R2​ would make a pole at 1/(2π⋅900 kΩ⋅200 pF)=8841/(2\pi \cdot 900\ \text{k}\Omega \cdot 200\ \text{pF}) = 8841/(2π⋅900 kΩ⋅200 pF)=884 Hz: the probe would not even pass 1 kHz. The solution is a capacitor C1C_1C1​ in parallel with the probe's resistor. Frequencies in hertz, with ω=2πf\omega = 2\pi fω=2πf.

Away from compensation: the Bode plot and the square wave

The zero stays put at 804 Hz; C2C_2C2​ moves only the pole.

  • Small C2C_2C2​, overcompensated. With 100 pF, fp=1.45f_p = 1.45fp​=1.45 kHz: the zero comes first, and the magnitude rises from −20 dB to 20log⁡100.180=−14.920\log_{10} 0.180 = -14.920log10​0.180=−14.9 dB, with a positive phase between the two, like a phase lead network. At a 1 V edge, the output jumps to 0.180 V and comes down to 0.1 V: each edge overshoots the final level.
  • Large C2C_2C2​, undercompensated. With 300 pF, fp=549f_p = 549fp​=549 Hz: the pole comes first, the magnitude falls to −23.3 dB, and the phase is negative, like a phase lag network. The edge jumps only to 0.068 V and climbs slowly to 0.1 V: the edges come out rounded.

Compensating means making both dividers equal. The figure lets you change C2C_2C2​; type the exact value in the box, such as 198p or 150 pF.

Which capacitor is adjusted

In most passive ×10 probes, the adjustment screw is a trimmer capacitor in the box next to the BNC connector, in parallel with the oscilloscope's input: it is part of C2C_2C2​, as in the model. In some models, the adjustment is in the probe's body: there the trimmer is in parallel with R1R_1R1​ and adjusts C1C_1C1​, and more capacitance overcompensates. You adjust it watching the oscilloscope's own calibration square wave, usually at 1 kHz, until the edges are square. Each input has its own capacitance, so the probe must be compensated again whenever it is moved to another channel or oscilloscope. Real probes also have high-frequency adjustments and a lossy cable, outside this model.

In the converters

On the bench, the buck's ripple and the edges of the switching node are far above fpf_pfp​, where the probe's gain is C1/(C1+C2)C_1/(C_1 + C_2)C1​/(C1​+C2​), not 0.1. With the example probe at 100 pF, the ripple appears 80% larger; at 300 pF, 32% smaller, and an overshoot or a slow edge on the screen may come from the probe, not from the converter. Compensate the probe before comparing the bench measurement with the lab oscilloscope's simulated scenarios.
