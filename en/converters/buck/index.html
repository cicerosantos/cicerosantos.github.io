Synchronous buck 24 V → 9 V: worked example

The lab's buck, from the specifications to the firmware, with the numbers of every step of the trail and the four controllers simulated in QSPICE.

This is the converter that runs through the From the analog buck to the digital loop trail: a synchronous buck controlled by a microcontroller, simulated in QSPICE with the firmware in C. Each step below points to the note that explains the theory and shows the number it gives for this buck.

Specifications

Quantity Value
Input / output 24 V → 9 V
Switching 200 kHz, dead time of 50 ns
Inductor 22 µH, resistance of 30 mΩ
Capacitor 47 µF, ESR of 5 mΩ
Switches RonR_{on}Ron​ = 10 mΩ
Load 9 Ω (1 A) and 4.5 Ω (2 A)
ADC 12 bits, full scale of 20 V
Duty ratio from 0 to 0.9

Design summary. Voltage-mode control, with one ADC sample and one controller update per period. The default compensator is a 2p2z with crossover at 8 kHz, two zeros at 2 kHz (below the LC resonance, at 5 kHz) and a pole at 60 kHz. The computed duty applies in the next period, with trailing-edge PWM.

The power stage

The loop sees only RsR_sRs​; the board needs the losses, the temperatures and the parts. These notes do the sums for the buck at 2 A, with real 60 V switches in place of the model's ideal ones:

  • RMS current in the buck's capacitors. The input capacitor carries 0.99 A RMS (1.02 A with the input at 18 V), almost three times the output capacitor's 0.37 A.
  • Losses of the synchronous buck. 581 mW of losses and 96.88 % efficiency; with the upper bound of the reverse recovery, 845 mW and 95.52 %. QSPICE, with the switch's model, confirms the turn-on and shows the turn-off almost free of loss.
  • Dead time and switching in the synchronous buck. The high-side switch switches with loss at both edges, and the low-side one turns on at zero voltage. The 50 ns of dead time cost 23 mW in the body diode and take 0.25 V off the node's average voltage.
  • The buck inductor: core losses, Dowell and saturation. A powdered-iron core made for 200 kHz loses 24 mW, against 474 mW for a general-purpose material.
  • Thermal design and part selection for the buck. The high-side switch, which carries the switching, CossC_{oss}Coss​ and reverse recovery losses, sits at 55 °C, and the low-side one at 43 °C, with a 40 °C ambient, with only the board's copper.

The original PID has no derivative filter and crosses over with only 25° of margin. The 2p2z crosses a little lower, with 41°. The aggressive design pushes the crossover to 18 kHz, where the delay already eats 44.6°. The unstable one crosses at 25 kHz, beyond the frequency where the phase crosses −180°, and oscillates.

In the converters

In the simulation with the 2p2z, the load step from 1 to 2 A pulls the output down to 8.59 V, close to the 0.42 V estimate of step 2. With the PID, the minimum is 8.66 V, and with the aggressive design, 8.73 V: crossing over higher dips less, but the smaller phase margin shows up as ringing.

What changes in the boost

The lab's Synchronous boost 12 V → 48 V: worked example (12 V → 48 V, 100 kHz) has a right-half-plane zero: at 14 kHz at 1 A and at 7 kHz at 2 A. This zero removes phase like a delay and forces the crossover to stay low, at 1.5 kHz in the boost's 2p2z, against 8 kHz here. The buck has no such zero, because the duty ratio multiplies no state variable. The four-switch buck-boost has the zero too and, besides, a plant that changes with the input.

Exercise

If the load changes to 18 Ω (0.5 A), what happens to the plant's resonant peak, and where must the margins be checked?

Answer

QQQ rises from 7.1 to 9.6, and the peak goes from 17.0 to 19.7 dB above the DC gain. The loop gain rises near 5 kHz, and that is where, along with the crossover, the margins must be checked again in the Bode widget.
