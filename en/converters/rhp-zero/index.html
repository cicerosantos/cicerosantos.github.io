The right-half-plane zero

In the boost and the buck-boost, raising the duty first pulls the output down; this right-half-plane zero, at R·D'²/L, limits the crossover frequency.

First, see: The buck's transfer function Gvd(s)G_{vd}(s)Gvd​(s), Right-half-plane zero

In the buck, raising the duty raises the voltage applied to the LC filter, and the output rises. In the boost, the same action has two opposite effects. The main switch stays on longer, the inductor builds up more current, and the output will rise; but while the switch is on, the inductor is cut off from the output. At first, the inductor current is still the same, but it reaches the output during a smaller fraction of the period, D′−d^D' - \hat dD′−d^: the output receives Id^I\hat dId^ less and falls. Only later, when the inductor current grows, does it rise.

A response that starts in the opposite direction to where it ends is the signature of a right-half-plane zero.

Right-half-plane zero

A zero at s=+2πfRHPs = +2\pi f_{\mathrm{RHP}}s=+2πfRHP​, with fRHP>0f_{\mathrm{RHP}} > 0fRHP​>0. Like an ordinary zero, it makes the magnitude rise by 20 dB per decade above fRHPf_{\mathrm{RHP}}fRHP​; but, like a pole, it lags the phase by up to 90∘90^\circ90∘. In a step, it makes the response start in the wrong direction.

Why it limits the crossover

An ordinary zero in the left half-plane helps the compensator: it gives gain and phase lead. The right-half-plane zero gives the gain and takes the phase, like a delay. The compensator cannot cancel it, because that would take a pole in the right half-plane, an unstable controller. What is left is to cross well below it: at fcf_cfc​, it already takes arctan⁡(fc/fRHP)\arctan(f_c/f_{\mathrm{RHP}})arctan(fc​/fRHP​), and this lag adds to the firmware's (Computation and PWM delay). Rules of thumb say to cross between a third and a fifth of the zero in the worst case: at fRHP/3f_{\mathrm{RHP}}/3fRHP​/3, it takes 18.4° of phase; at fRHP/5f_{\mathrm{RHP}}/5fRHP​/5, 11.3°. Neither is a limit: what decides is the margin at the worst corner, adding up the zero, the delay and the lead the compensator still gives there (Robust design across the operating range).

And the worst case is the maximum load with the minimum input: smaller RRR and D′D'D′, lower zero. Unlike the buck's, the boost's plant changes a lot with the operating point, and the margins must be checked at all of them (Loop gain, crossover frequency and margins).

More gain does not make the boost faster

With a slow boost, the temptation is to raise the gain. Near the zero, the phase falls fast (26.6° at fRHP/2f_{\mathrm{RHP}}/2fRHP​/2, 45° at it), the margin disappears, and the loop starts ringing on the load step, precisely where the current is highest and the zero lowest.

The ADC does not read the average

In the boost, the ADC samples just before the main switch turns on, when the capacitor carries the whole inductor current, and the sample includes an ESR term (Sampling instant and the ESR in the boost). The site's model accounts for it, and with it the zero of the plant the ADC sees is higher (about 8.4 kHz at 2 A). For design, the zero of the average, lower, is the conservative reference.
