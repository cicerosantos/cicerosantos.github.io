Duty ratio and ripple of the buck in CCM

In steady state, the volt-second balance on the inductor gives the buck's duty ratio, and the triangular current gives the current and voltage ripple.

The buck connects the switching node to the input, then to ground, and the LC filter delivers the average of that square wave to the load. This note computes the duty ratio and the ripple in steady state. It is the first step of the digital buck trail.

Volt-second balance

In steady state, the inductor current ends each period at the value it started with. Since vL=L diL/dtv_L = L\,di_L/dtvL​=LdiL​/dt, the area of the inductor voltage over one period is zero:

Duty ratio

The duty ratio DDD is the fraction of the switching period Ts=1/fsT_s = 1/f_sTs​=1/fs​ during which the high-side switch conducts. We write D′=1−DD' = 1 - DD′=1−D for the rest of the period.

The resistive losses (Rs=RL+RonR_s = R_L + R_{on}Rs​=RL​+Ron​, in series with the inductor) call for slightly more DDD, because part of the voltage drops across them. In closed loop this does not matter: the controller finds the DDD that gives VoV_oVo​ on its own.

The boundary of continuous conduction

Continuous conduction mode (CCM)

In CCM, the inductor current never rests at zero during the period. The boundary is Io=ΔiL/2I_o = \Delta i_L/2Io​=ΔiL​/2: below it, the valley of the triangular wave would reach zero.

In a buck with a diode, below the boundary the diode blocks and the converter enters discontinuous conduction (DCM), with another duty ratio and other dynamics. The synchronous buck is different: the low-side switch conducts in both directions, the current goes negative in the valley and the converter stays in CCM, called forced CCM. The equations of this note hold at any load.

Negative current in the synchronous buck

At light load, or in a downward reference step, the negative current returns energy to the input. A bench supply that cannot sink current may see its voltage rise. Controllers with "diode emulation" turn the low-side switch off when the current reaches zero, and the converter then enters DCM again.

Output voltage ripple

The capacitor absorbs the AC part of iLi_LiL​, a triangle of ±ΔiL/2\pm\Delta i_L/2±ΔiL​/2. The capacitor's series resistance adds a part proportional to the current itself:

ΔvC=ΔiL8 C fs,ΔvESR=RESR ΔiL(3)\Delta v_C = \frac{\Delta i_L}{8\,C\,f_s}, \qquad \Delta v_{\mathrm{ESR}} = R_{\mathrm{ESR}}\,\Delta i_L\tag{3}ΔvC​=8Cfs​ΔiL​​,ΔvESR​=RESR​ΔiL​(3)

The two parts are out of phase: the ESR's follows the current, and the capacitive one is its integral, with peaks a quarter period apart. The total ripple lies between the larger one and their sum. In the converters

The lab's buck goes from 24 V to 9 V at 200 kHz, with L = 22 µH and C = 47 µF (ESR 5 mΩ).

In the QSPICE simulation, before the load step, each period shows ΔiL≈1.28\Delta i_L \approx 1.28ΔiL​≈1.28 A and about 18 mV of output ripple. The ADC samples at the start of the period, in the current's valley. With ideal waveforms, that point sits about 6 mV below the average; in the simulation it is about 5 mV, one ADC step (see Sampling and the zero-order hold (ZOH) and ADC quantization).
