Dead time and switching in the synchronous buck

What happens at the switch node during the dead time, why the high-side switch switches with loss and the low-side one does not, and how to choose the dead time between shoot-through and body-diode loss.

First, see: Losses of the synchronous buck

In the synchronous buck, the two switches must never conduct together: that would short the input. The driver turns one switch off and waits a dead time before turning the other on. In that interval, the inductor current does not stop, and the path it finds decides who switches with loss.

The two body diodes

Body diode

The body diode is the drain-to-source junction every power MOSFET has, in parallel with the channel. It conducts from source to drain whenever the voltage across the switch goes negative, with a drop VSDV_{SD}VSD​ between 0.7 and 1 V.

With the inductor current positive at both edges, the period runs in this sequence:

  1. The high-side switch turns off at the peak current. The inductor discharges the node's capacitances, and the node falls from VinV_{in}Vin​ to zero. While the channel closes, the switch still has voltage and current: that is the turn-off loss.
  2. The low-side switch's body diode conducts until the end of the dead time, with the node at −VSD-V_{SD}−VSD​.
  3. The low-side switch turns on with almost zero volts across it. There is no switching loss: this is zero-voltage switching (ZVS).
  4. The low-side switch turns off at the valley. The current, still positive, goes back into the body diode.
  5. The high-side switch turns on against the conducting diode. It must take over the valley current, charge the node from zero to VinV_{in}Vin​ and remove the charge stored in the diode.

Reverse recovery

A junction diode that has conducted holds charge. To block, it conducts in reverse until that charge is removed. The removed charge is QrrQ_{rr}Qrr​, and the reverse recovery costs QrrVinQ_{rr} V_{in}Qrr​Vin​ per period, dissipated in the switch that turns on.

That is why the high-side switch switches with loss at both edges, and the low-side one at neither. The losses of Losses of the synchronous buck follow this picture.

In step 4, the current flows from the output into the node, charges the capacitances up to VinV_{in}Vin​ and puts the high-side switch's body diode into conduction. If the dead time is long enough for that, the high-side switch also turns on at zero voltage. The time needed is the node's charge divided by the valley current.

Choosing the dead time

Minimum. The dead time must cover the delay mismatch between the driver's two channels, the fall time of the switch turning off and a margin. With less, both switches conduct together every period.

Maximum. Every extra nanosecond is diode time, with VSDV_{SD}VSD​ in place of IRonI R_{on}IRon​, and a diode that conducts longer accumulates more charge for the reverse recovery. The dead time also changes the node's average voltage. At the edge where the high-side switch turns on, the node sits at −VSD-V_{SD}−VSD​ instead of VinV_{in}Vin​, and the average loses (Vin+VSD) tdt fs(V_{in} + V_{SD})\,t_{dt}\,f_s(Vin​+VSD​)tdt​fs​; at the other, it sits at −VSD-V_{SD}−VSD​ instead of −IRon-I R_{on}−IRon​, almost nothing.

The edges

The current the switch interrupts changes in a loop with parasitic inductance: the input capacitor, the two switches and the traces between them (RMS current in the buck's capacitors). Exercise

At a 1 A load, does the high-side switch turn on at zero voltage with the 50 ns dead time?

Answer

No. At 1 A, the valley is 1−1.28/2=0.361 - 1.28/2 = 0.361−1.28/2=0.36 A, positive: the current goes into the low-side switch's diode and does not charge the node up to VinV_{in}Vin​. ZVS of the high-side switch only appears below 0.64 A, with a negative valley.

Exercise

If the power loop's inductance fell from 17 to 5 nH, with the same di/dtdi/dtdi/dt, what would the voltage spike across the switch be?

Answer

5 nH⋅0.38 A/ns=1.95\,\text{nH}\cdot 0.38\,\text{A/ns} = 1.95nH⋅0.38A/ns=1.9 V, and the peak would be 25.9 V instead of 30.4 V. The ringing would rise to 1/(2π5 nH⋅520 pF)=991/(2\pi\sqrt{5\,\text{nH}\cdot 520\,\text{pF}}) = 991/(2π5nH⋅520pF​)=99 MHz.
