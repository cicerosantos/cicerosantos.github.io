The ESR zero

The output capacitor's series resistance adds a zero to the plant; with electrolytics it helps the loop, with ceramics it lies above the switching frequency and does not count.

ESR zero

The ESR zero is the frequency at which the two parts of the capacitor's impedance have equal magnitude. Above it, the buck's plant falls 20 dB per decade instead of 40, and the phase rises by up to 90° relative to the pole pair alone.

What the zero does on the Bode plot

A left-half-plane zero lifts the phase: arctan⁡(f/fESR)\arctan(f/f_{\mathrm{ESR}})arctan(f/fESR​), which is 45° at fESRf_{\mathrm{ESR}}fESR​ and almost nothing a decade below. If the zero is near the crossover fcf_cfc​, it supplies for free part of the phase boost the compensator would have to give. That was the case in older bucks with electrolytic capacitors, whose ESR is tens of milliohms: with them, a type II compensator was enough.

With ceramic capacitors, the ESR drops to a few milliohms, and the zero moves up to hundreds of kHz, above fs/2f_s/2fs​/2. There it does not help: the digital loop does not even see frequencies above fs/2f_s/2fs​/2 (see Sampling and the zero-order hold (ZOH)), and the compensator must give all the phase boost, with two zeros, like the The 2p2z compensator.

Do not count on the ESR

An electrolytic's ESR varies with temperature (it may double in the cold) and grows with aging. A design that relies on the ESR zero for its phase margin may lose it in winter or after a few years. And putting a ceramic in parallel, to lower the ripple, moves the zero and changes the whole plant.

ESR on purpose

A resistor is sometimes added in series with the capacitor just to create the zero. It works, but the cost shows in the output ripple and in the load-step response, which gains an instantaneous jump of ΔI⋅RESR\Delta I\cdot R_{\mathrm{ESR}}ΔI⋅RESR​.

In the converters

In the lab's buck, C = 47 µF with an ESR of 5 mΩ:

fESR=12π⋅5 mΩ⋅47 µF=677 kHzf_{\mathrm{ESR}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\cdot 5\,\text{m}\Omega\cdot 47\,\text{µF}} = 677\ \text{kHz}fESR​=2π⋅5mΩ⋅47µF1​=677 kHz

That is 3.4 times the 200 kHz switching frequency. At 8 kHz, the 2p2z's crossover, the zero gives 0.7° of phase; even at fs/2f_s/2fs​/2 = 100 kHz it gives only 8.4°. For the loop, the capacitor is ideal, and the compensator does all the work.

In the boost and the buck-boost, C = 100 µF with an ESR of 10 mΩ, and the zero lies at 159 kHz, also above the 100 kHz switching frequency. There the ESR shows up another way: the ADC samples just before switch G1 turns on, when the capacitor carries the whole inductor current, and reads RESRR_{\mathrm{ESR}}RESR​ times that current above the average. The model validated in QSPICE includes this term (Sampling instant and the ESR in the boost).
