Soft-start and pre-biasing

The reference ramp, which avoids saturation during the transition, and pre-biasing, where the output already has voltage before the converter starts switching.

First, see: Anti-windup and duty saturation

In Anti-windup and duty saturation, a reference step saturates the duty, and the derivative kick is one of the triggers. The best saturation is the one that doesn't happen. This note covers two situations in which the output does not start where the controller expects: the reference ramp (soft-start), which makes the transition without saturating, and pre-biasing, when the output already has voltage before the converter starts switching.

1. The reference ramp

With a ramp on the reference, the error stays small during the transition, and the compensator does not saturate. With the 50 V/ms ramp, the reference moves 0.250.250.25 V per switching period, which is 5 µs at 200 kHz. In the firmware, at each interrupt the reference in use moves a limited step toward the target, as in scenario.cpp:

float step = SLEW * 1e3f * TS;                /* V per period; SLEW in V/ms, TS in s */
float change = vref_target - vref;
if (change > step) change = step;
if (change < -step) change = -step;
vref += change;

A soft-start uses the same ramp, from zero up to the steady-state value; there is no recorded scenario of it.

2. Pre-biasing

Pre-biasing is an output that already has voltage when the converter starts switching, for example because the output capacitor was charged by another path. It changes three things compared with an output that starts discharged:

  • the initial error is the reference minus the voltage that already exists. With synchronous rectification, a ramp from zero makes the controller discharge the output; with diode emulation, the converter just idles until the ramp reaches the existing voltage;
  • with synchronous rectification, the low-side switch conducts for the rest of each period, and the average inductor voltage is D Vin−VoD\,V_{in} - V_oDVin​−Vo​. While it is negative, the current falls, goes negative and stays negative for a while after, like a ringing LC, with a peak of up to Vo,pre/L/CV_{o,\text{pre}}/\sqrt{L/C}Vo,pre​/L/C​. This current discharges the capacitor and returns energy to the input, which can raise VinV_{in}Vin​ if the source cannot sink current. Releasing the low-side switch only after the first pulse is not enough;
  • the integrator starts at zero, but in steady state the buck needs D=Vo/VinD = V_o/V_{in}D=Vo​/Vin​ (Duty ratio and ripple of the buck in CCM).

There are two ways out. The first is to start with D≈Vo/VinD \approx V_o/V_{in}D≈Vo​/Vin​: the ramp starts from the measured voltage and the integrator starts with the duty that holds that voltage. The ramp alone is not enough. The second is to keep the rectifier in diode emulation, with the low-side switch turned off when the current reaches zero, until the converter enters CCM, that is, until a whole period passes without the current reaching zero. The D≈Vo/VinD \approx V_o/V_{in}D≈Vo​/Vin​ threshold holds only in CCM; in DCM, the regulating duty is smaller.

Two practical cautions. With the output pre-biased and the low-side switch off, the high-side switch's bootstrap capacitor may not charge; controllers give short low-side pulses before the first pulse. And diode emulation removes no charge from the output: an overvoltage, or a pre-bias above the target, only goes down through the load. In the boost, the output already starts near VinV_{in}Vin​, and the threshold is D≈1−Vin/VoD \approx 1 - V_{in}/V_oD≈1−Vin​/Vo​; in the four-switch buck-boost, D≈Vo/(Vin+Vo)D \approx V_o/(V_{in}+V_o)D≈Vo​/(Vin​+Vo​).

In the buck, with 22 µH and 47 µF, L/C=22/47 Ω≈0.68 Ω\sqrt{L/C} = \sqrt{22/47}\ \Omega \approx 0.68\ \OmegaL/C​=22/47​ Ω≈0.68 Ω: the peak can reach about 1.5 A per volt of pre-bias. This section describes the mechanism, which was not simulated in QSPICE.

In the converters

Buck. In the pid-vref scenario, the reference goes from 9 to 5 V with the 50 V/ms ramp, the same descent that Derivative kick and derivative on the measurement shows without a ramp. Nothing saturates: the inductor current stops at −2.5 A, and the output falls at most 35 mV below 5 V.

∿Open in the oscilloscope: Buck, the same descent from 9 to 5 V with the reference ramp: no saturation, and the inductor current stops at −2.5 A.

Boost. The pid-vref scenario goes from 48 to 40 V with the 10 V/ms ramp. Eight volts take 0.8 ms, or 80 periods of 10 µs, and the output lags it by 0.8 to 2.1 V, reaching 40.5 V 1.8 ms after the step. In the pid-vref and 2p2z-vref scenarios, the duty, measured as the fraction of each period with G1 on, stays between 0.70 and 0.75, below the 0.85 limit: nothing saturates (Synchronous boost 12 V → 48 V: worked example).

∿Open in the oscilloscope: Boost, reference ramp from 48 to 40 V: the output lags the ramp by up to 2.1 V.

Buck-boost. The pid-vref scenario rises from 12 to 15 V with the 10 V/ms ramp, and the output goes above the input. Three volts take 0.3 ms, or 30 periods of 10 µs (Four-switch buck-boost 8–16 V → 12 V: worked example).

Exercise 1

In the buck, with the 50 V/ms ramp, how long does the descent from 9 to 5 V take, and what is the PID's derivative term (differentiating the error) in the ramp's first period?

Answer

4 V/(50 V/ms)=80 μ4\ \text{V}/(50\ \text{V/ms}) = 80\ \mu4 V/(50 V/ms)=80 μs, or 16 periods. In the ramp's first period the error changes by −0.25 V, and the derivative is KD×(−0.25)/Ts=0.3×(−0.25)=−0.075K_D \times (-0.25)/T_s = 0.3 \times (-0.25) = -0.075KD​×(−0.25)/Ts​=0.3×(−0.25)=−0.075, against −1.2 for the step in Derivative kick and derivative on the measurement. After that, with the output following the ramp, the derivative stays near zero.

Exercise 2

In the boost, the reference goes from 48 to 40 V with the 10 V/ms ramp. How many switching periods does the ramp last?

Answer

8 V/(10 V/ms)=0.88\ \text{V}/(10\ \text{V/ms}) = 0.88 V/(10 V/ms)=0.8 ms. With 100 kHz, the period is 10 µs, so there are 80 periods.
