Robust design across the operating range

One fixed compensator for a plant that changes with the input and the load: the corners of the range, the crossover between about 1/5 and 1/3 of the right-half-plane zero in the worst case, and the margins checked at every point.

First, see: Loop gain, crossover frequency and margins, The right-half-plane zero

In the buck, the plant hardly changes with the load, and one design point is enough (The buck's transfer function Gvd(s)G_{vd}(s)Gvd​(s)). In the boost and the buck-boost, the resonance, the gain above it and the right-half-plane zero depend on D′D'D′ and on the current (Transfer function Gvd(s)G_{vd}(s)Gvd​(s) of the boost, Four-switch buck-boost). The firmware has a single compensator, with fixed coefficients, and it must work across the whole range.

1. List the corners of the range

The extremes of the input and the load form the corners: minimum and maximum input, minimum and maximum load. Add the nominal point, where the gain will be tuned.

2. Find the worst right-half-plane zero

The zero comes down when the load rises and when the input falls (equation Right-half-plane zero of the boost; in the buck-boost, equation Right-half-plane zero of the buck-boost). The worst case is the minimum input with the maximum load. That zero, not the nominal point's, limits the crossover.

3. Choose the crossover

At fcf_cfc​, the zero takes arctan⁡(fc/fRHP)\arctan(f_c/f_{\mathrm{RHP}})arctan(fc​/fRHP​) of phase, and the firmware's delay takes 360∘ fc td360^\circ\,f_c\,t_d360∘fc​td​ (Computation and PWM delay). Rules of thumb ask for fcf_cfc​ between fRHP,min/5f_{\mathrm{RHP,min}}/5fRHP,min​/5 (the zero costs 11.3°) and fRHP,min/3f_{\mathrm{RHP,min}}/3fRHP,min​/3 (18.4°). They are starting points: the phase budget of the loop gain at the worst corner decides. In the lab's boost, lowering the crossover from 1/3 to 1/5 of the worst zero by the gain alone doesn't improve the phase margin (35.2–35.6°): the 2p2z's zeros, fixed at 500 Hz, stop giving phase. Only the gain margin improves, from 10.0 to 18.0 dB.

4. Place the zeros and the pole

The two zeros of the 2p2z go below the lowest resonance of the range, so that they already give phase when the plant's phase plunges. The pole goes well above the crossover.

5. Tune the gain and follow the crossover

The gain KKK is tuned at the nominal point; at the other points, the crossover moves. Well above the resonance, the plant tends to Vin/((2πf)2LC)V_{in}/((2\pi f)^2 LC)Vin​/((2πf)2LC): in the boost and the buck-boost, it is the DC gain, about Vin/D′2V_{in}/D'^2Vin​/D′2, times (f0/f)2(f_0/f)^2(f0​/f)2, with f0f_0f0​ proportional to D′D'D′, and the D′2D'^2D′2 cancels. Higher input, higher crossover. Near the resonance, the plant lies above the asymptote, and since f0f_0f0​ rises with the input, the crossover rises more than it predicts.

6. Check the margins at every corner

A single point misleads: the margin can be comfortable at light load and almost nil at heavy load.

The load step leads to the worst point

On a load step, the converter moves to the point of larger current and lower zero. With little margin there, the ringing shows up right after the step, when the loop is needed.

Exercise

A colleague approves the buck-boost design by the 42.6° at the nominal point, 12 V and 2 A. Using the table, which point did he not see, and with what margin?

Answer

The worst corner is 9 V and 3 A, with 39.1° against 42.6°. The smallest gain margin is at another corner: 11.7 dB at 15 V and 3 A, against 13.8 dB. The 3.5° look small, but the aggressive design falls to 2.7° at the same corner.
