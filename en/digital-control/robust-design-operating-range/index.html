Robust design across the operating range

One fixed compensator for a plant that changes with the input and the load: the corners of the range, the crossover between about 1/5 and 1/3 of the right-half-plane zero in the worst case, and the margins checked at every point.

First, see: Loop gain, crossover frequency and margins, The right-half-plane zero

In the buck, the plant hardly changes with the load, and one design point is enough (The buck's transfer function Gvd(s)G_{vd}(s)Gvd​(s)). In the boost and the buck-boost, the resonance, the gain above it and the right-half-plane zero depend on D′D'D′ and on the current (Transfer function Gvd(s)G_{vd}(s)Gvd​(s) of the boost, Four-switch buck-boost). The firmware has a single compensator, with fixed coefficients. This note shows how to choose that compensator so that it works across the whole range.

1. List the corners of the range

The plant changes with the input and with the load, and the extremes of each form the corners: minimum and maximum input, minimum and maximum load. Add the nominal point, where the gain will be tuned. The margins are computed at all these points, at the end.

2. Find the worst right-half-plane zero

The zero comes down when the load rises and when the input falls (equation Right-half-plane zero of the boost; in the buck-boost, equation Right-half-plane zero of the buck-boost). The worst case is the minimum input with the maximum load. That zero, not the nominal point's, limits the crossover.

They are starting points: what decides is the phase budget of the loop gain at the worst corner. In the lab's boost, lowering the crossover from 1/3 to 1/5 of the worst zero by the gain alone, without moving the 2p2z's zeros, leaves the phase margin at 34.5–34.7°: what the zero and the delay give back, the compensator, with its zeros fixed at 500 Hz, stops giving. Only gain margin is gained (from 9.8 to 17.8 dB).

4. Place the zeros and the pole

The two zeros of the 2p2z go below the lowest resonance of the range, so that they already give phase when the plant's phase plunges. The pole goes well above the crossover.

5. Tune the gain and follow the crossover

The gain KKK is tuned at the nominal point. At the other points, the crossover moves. Well above the resonance, the plant's magnitude tends to Vin/(ω2LC)V_{in}/(\omega^2 LC)Vin​/(ω2LC) in the buck, the boost and the buck-boost. In the last two, it is the DC gain, about Vin/D′2V_{in}/D'^2Vin​/D′2, times (f0/f)2(f_0/f)^2(f0​/f)2, with f0f_0f0​ proportional to D′D'D′: the D′2D'^2D′2 cancels. Higher input, higher crossover. Near the resonance, the plant lies above that asymptote, and further above the closer it is: since f0f_0f0​ rises with the input, the crossover rises more than the asymptote predicts.

6. Check the margins at every corner

The margin of a single point misleads. A design can have a comfortable margin at light load and almost none at heavy load.

The load step leads to the worst point

On a load step, the converter moves precisely to the point of larger current and lower zero. If the margin there is small, the ringing shows up right after the step, when the loop is needed.

Exercise

By the 1/5 rule, how high can the buck-boost's crossover go at 9 V and 3 A? And at 8 V and 3 A, where the zero is at 7.0 kHz?

Answer

8.5/5=1.78.5/5 = 1.78.5/5=1.7 kHz and 7.0/5=1.47.0/5 = 1.47.0/5=1.4 kHz. The 2p2z crosses at 2.40 and 2.20 kHz at those points, above the rule, with 37.9° and 35.9° of margin. The 1/5 rule is conservative; the 1/3 rule, used by other sources, admits both points (2.8 and 2.3 kHz). Whoever crosses above 1/5 must check the phase budget at every corner.
