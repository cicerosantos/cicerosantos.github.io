PWM timer and shadow registers

The PWM timer counts up to the period and compares with the CCR; the new value enters through the preload, at the update event. Edge, center, dead time and minimum pulse decide what the duty cycle becomes on the board, and DMIN = 0 in the model hides those limits.

First, see: PWM update: trailing edge vs symmetric

A digital PWM is a timer that counts clock pulses, and the output switches when the count passes the compare value. What comes out on the board also depends on the instant the new value takes effect and on the limits of the switch and the driver.

Counter, ARR and CCR

On a general-purpose timer, the counter counts from 0 up to the value in the ARR register and returns to zero, so the period has ARR + 1 counts. A compare register, the CCR, says at which count the output changes. With the trailing edge, the output switches on at the start of the period and off at the CCR. The duty cycle is d=CCR/NPWMd = \mathrm{CCR}/N_{\mathrm{PWM}}d=CCR/NPWM​, with NPWMN_{\mathrm{PWM}}NPWM​ counts per period, as in DPWM resolution and limit cycles. In the HRTIM, those roles belong to PERxR and CMP1xR.

On the H7 reference, the HRTIM counts at 400 MHz: at 200 kHz there are 2000 counts per period, each 2.5 ns long; on a general-purpose timer, that is ARR = 1999. The HRTIM period and compare values must be at least 3 periods of the fHRTIMf_{\mathrm{HRTIM}}fHRTIM​ clock: with CKPSC = 101, CMP1xR ≥ 3 (RM0433, table 308). The CMP1xR = 0 that DMIN = 0 asks for is out of range: clamp it to 3 and, to switch the output off, use HRTIM_ODISR, which takes it to its idle state.

Another compare channel, placed at a chosen count, triggers the ADC conversion. In the HRTIM, the registers HRTIM_ADC1R to HRTIM_ADC4R select those events (RM0433, section 37.3.18); in HRTIM_ADC1R, for timer A, they are the bits AD1TAC2 to AD1TAC4, AD1TAPER and AD1TARST. CMP1 of timers A to E cannot trigger the ADC; the master timer's CMP1 can (AD1MC1).

Preload and update event

Without preload, a write to the CCR takes effect at once, and what comes out depends on the timer. On a general-purpose timer in level PWM mode, a new CCR below the current count drops the output at once, and the pulse comes out short; a larger CCR written after the fall turns the output back on, and the pulse comes out doubled. In the HRTIM, the output is set and reset by events: a CMP below the count misses the reset event, and the switch stays on until the next period's reset. That is the dangerous case.

With preload (PREEN in HRTIM_TIMxCR on the H7; OC1PE on a general-purpose timer), the write goes to the shadow register, and the update event copies the value into the active register. In the HRTIM, the transfer needs an enabled update source, such as the TxRSTU or TxREPU bits in HRTIM_TIMxCR (HRTIM_TIMCR_TRSTU and HRTIM_TIMCR_TREPU in CMSIS), or a software update, the TASWU bit in HRTIM_CR2 for timer A (RM0433, section 37.3.10). A value written at kTskT_skTs​ takes effect in the next period: this is the one-period delay of Computation and PWM delay.

In trailing-edge PWM, the update event is the start of the period, and only the falling edge moves. In center-aligned PWM, it is the peak, the valley or both, and both edges move (PWM update: trailing edge vs symmetric); the H7 HRTIM counter only counts up, so the centered pulse comes from two comparators (Centered PWM on the H7: case B). The ARR also has preload.

Bootstrap recharge

The high-side switch's driver usually feeds the gate from a bootstrap capacitor, recharged only while the low-side switch conducts. The recharge window in each period may stand for the upper duty-cycle limit. The recharge time comes from the driver's datasheet.

Minimum pulse and DMIN = 0

There is a shortest pulse that the timer and the driver can deliver: if DTsDT_sDTs​ does not exceed the dead time, the high-side switch never conducts, and the driver has its own minimum response time, which comes from its datasheet. The averaged model and the clamp at DMIN = 0 have no lower limit: a request for 10−310^{-3}10−3 passes as a real output. The switched simulation already swallows pulses shorter than the dead time: with D=10−3D = 10^{-3}D=10−3, 5 ns against 50 ns, G1 never turns on, as on a board. The driver's own minimum pulse is not modeled.

In the converters

Buck. At 200 kHz, 2000 counts per period. DMAX may stand for the bootstrap recharge; DMIN stands for no minimum pulse at all.

Boost. At 100 kHz, the period has 4000 counts. The controlled switch, G1, is on the ground side. The high-side one, G2, which rectifies, uses a bootstrap recharged while G1 conducts: that imposes a minimum duty cycle on the synchronous boost. The range from 0 to 0.85 does not include the minimum pulse either.
