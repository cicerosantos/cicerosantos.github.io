ADC quantization

The ADC reads the output in steps of q=VFS/(2N−1)q = V_{FS}/(2^N - 1)q=VFS​/(2N−1): the step, the quantization noise, the reference written as a code and the sampling instant decide what the loop can regulate.

The digital controller doesn't see the output voltage: it sees an integer. In Sampling and the zero-order hold (ZOH), sampling made time discrete; here the A/D converter (ADC) makes the amplitude discrete, and everything the firmware knows about vov_ovo​ goes through that rounding.

The step (LSB)

ADC step

The step, or LSB (least significant bit), is the smallest change of the measured voltage that changes the code read. An NNN-bit ADC has 2N2^N2N codes, from 0 to 2N−12^N - 12N−1: code 0 reads 0 V, the largest reads full scale, and between them the measuring range is divided into 2N−12^N - 12N−1 steps.

In steady state it fails: the output barely changes, and the quantization error becomes a fixed error, or a repeating pattern. The same goes for a measurement: a signal that moves the sample by less than one code is lost in the rounding, which is why the sine that measures the loop gain grows with frequency (Measuring the loop gain by injecting into the duty).

What the zero-error band means

For the controller, every output within a band of width qqq around the reference's code gives the same error. Inside that band the integrator has nothing to correct. The regulation's resolution is, at best, ±q/2=±2.44\pm q/2 = \pm 2.44±q/2=±2.44 mV at 9 V, or 0.03 %.

That is resolution, not accuracy. The divider's tolerance and the ADC's gain and offset errors are worth tens of steps (see exercise 1). And the loop only settles inside the band if the duty has a level that puts the output there: if the digital PWM is coarser than the ADC, the output jumps over the band and the loop oscillates, which is the subject of DPWM resolution and limit cycles.

The reference as a code

The firmware compares numbers. If the reference is written in volts, it almost never coincides with a code. With q=20/4095q = 20/4095q=20/4095 V, 9 V is 9⋅4095/20=1842.759 \cdot 4095/20 = 1842.759⋅4095/20=1842.75 codes: the ADC only reads 1842 (8.9963 V) or 1843 (9.0012 V), and the error is +3.66+3.66+3.66 mV or −1.22-1.22−1.22 mV, never zero. The integrator goes up and down between the two codes forever.

Write the reference as a code

Compute the reference once, as an integer: REF_CODE = 1843, the code nearest to 1842.75. The loop then regulates at 1843⋅20/4095=9.00121843 \cdot 20/4095 = 9.00121843⋅20/4095=9.0012 V, 1.2 mV above 9 V, a quarter of a step. The error becomes REF_CODE - adc, an integer that can be exactly zero.

The sampling instant

The output has switching ripple, and the loop regulates the sampled value, not the average: sampling always at the same phase of the period, the difference becomes a constant offset. The simulations sample at the start of each period, as in Sampling and the zero-order hold (ZOH).

Sampling at the switching edge

At the start of the period the high-side switch turns on, and on the board the measured voltage rings for tens to hundreds of nanoseconds, from the layout's parasitic inductances and the MOSFETs' capacitances. In the simulation the sample is clean; on the board, it is the worst instant. Trigger the ADC from the timer at a compare point away from the edges, for example in the middle of the on or off time, and check it on the scope.

High-impedance divider

The ADC's sampling capacitor charges through the divider during the sampling window. The maximum external impedance is 50 kΩ, and the internal sampling capacitor is 4 pF (DS12110, table 77): with 50 kΩ, the RC constant is 200 ns, more than the minimum window of 1.5 cycles (42 ns at 36 MHz). With larger resistors, such as hundreds of kΩ, and a short window, it doesn't charge fully and the code comes out low. Use a capacitor at the pin, or a longer window. An RC filter at the pin also attenuates noise, but adds phase to the loop, like the delay of Computation and PWM delay.
