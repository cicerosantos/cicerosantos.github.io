Sampling and the zero-order hold (ZOH)

The ADC reads the output once per period, at the same point of the PWM, and the computed duty is held until the next update; this removes the ripple, creates an offset and delays the response: half a period in the ZOH model, DTsDT_sDTs​ in this site's trailing-edge PWM.

First, see: Loop gain, crossover frequency and margins

In an analog loop, the compensator sees the output voltage all the time. In a microcontroller, the ADC takes one sample per period, triggered by the PWM's own timer, and the firmware only knows the sequence v[k]=vo(kTs)v[k] = v_o(kT_s)v[k]=vo​(kTs​). On this site, the sample is taken at the start of each PWM period, just before the main switch, G1, turns on, and the sampling period is the switching period: Ts=1/fsT_s = 1/f_sTs​=1/fs​. By default the sample sits on the switching edge; on a board, it moves away from there (ADC trigger and sample instant).

Nyquist

A sampled sequence cannot tell a sine at fff from one at fs−ff_s - ffs​−f, fs+ff_s + ffs​+f and so on: they all give the same samples. Only the band up to the Nyquist frequency, fs/2f_s/2fs​/2, is represented without ambiguity. Any component above it shows up folded (aliased) below it, and the loop reacts to it as if it were real. That is why the site's Bode plots stop at fs/2f_s/2fs​/2, and the crossover sits many times below it (Loop gain, crossover frequency and margins).

The ripple and the offset

The switching ripple is exactly at fsf_sfs​ and its harmonics. Sampled at fsf_sfs​, it folds down to zero frequency: since the ADC always reads the same point of the period, every sample sees the same part of the ripple. The result is a smooth signal, with no ripple, which is good for control, but shifted from the average by a constant.

Sampling offset

It is the difference between the ripple's value at the sampling instant and the output's average. The controller's integrator drives the error of the sample to zero, so the output's average ends up shifted from the reference by this offset.

In the buck, with the switch turning on at the start of the period, the inductor current is at its minimum there: the capacitor is discharging, and the ESR adds a drop. The sample reads below the average, and the output's average sits a little above the reference. The value depends on DDD, on the load and on any delay in the ADC trigger.

Sampling near an edge

On the board, right after each switching edge there are spikes and ringing of tens of MHz at the output node and in the divider. A sample at the edge can catch that noise. On microcontrollers, delay the ADC trigger a few hundred nanoseconds after the edge, or sample in the middle of the on pulse: in a symmetric PWM, it is centered on the carrier's valley, where the sample falls (PWM update: trailing edge vs symmetric). The anti-aliasing filter (Sensing and anti-aliasing filter) is for those spikes, not for correcting the offset. In the boost and the buck-boost, "after the edge" already changes the plant (ADC trigger and sample instant).

The ZOH is the classic model of a D/A converter. In a power converter, what holds the duty is the PWM, and what matters is when the modulated edge moves. In a trailing-edge PWM, the information enters at the edge that comes DTsDT_sDTs​ after the start of the period, and the modulator's delay is DTsDT_sDTs​, not Ts/2T_s/2Ts​/2; with D=0.5D = 0.5D=0.5 the two agree. In the converters

In the buck, fsf_sfs​ = 200 kHz and the ADC has 12 bits and a full scale of 20 V referred to the output. The Nyquist frequency is 100 kHz, and the loop crosses at 8 kHz.

In the boost and the buck-boost, the sample is also taken just before the main switch turns on, when the capacitor carries the whole inductor current. The small-signal model of those converters includes this ESR term, which changes the plant the ADC sees (Sampling instant and the ESR in the boost).

190 kHz folds to 200−190=10200 - 190 = 10200−190=10 kHz, inside the loop's band. The 200 kHz ripple folds to 200−200=0200 - 200 = 0200−200=0: it becomes a constant, the sampling offset.
