DPWM resolution and limit cycles

The duty also moves in steps: when the step it causes at the output is larger than the ADC step, the loop has nowhere to settle and oscillates in a limit cycle.

First, see: ADC quantization

In ADC quantization, the measurement of the output moved in steps. So does the actuator: the digital PWM (DPWM) only produces a finite number of duty values, and the two quantizers together can keep the loop from settling.

Limit cycle

Limit cycle

A sustained periodic oscillation of a nonlinear system, with no external excitation. In a quantized loop, the output alternates between DPWM levels because none of them cancels the error read.

The mechanism is simple. The integrator accumulates the error and raises the duty one step; the output rises 12 mV and jumps over the 4.884 mV band; the error changes sign, the integrator goes down, and the story repeats. The linear analysis of Loop gain, crossover frequency and margins doesn't predict it: the loop can have good margins and still oscillate, with an amplitude of the order of the DPWM step.

Peterchev and Sanders (2003) gave three conditions for removing steady-state limit cycles:

  1. a DPWM finer than the ADC, so that a DPWM level exists inside the zero-error band. They suggest one extra bit, which gives two DPWM levels per ADC level;
  2. an integrator in the controller, with limited gain. Without it, an error remains in steady state and the resting point doesn't fall inside the band; with too much gain, it can't adjust the duty in fine steps. With the variables normalized to VinV_{in}Vin​, the integral gain lies between 0 and 1;
  3. no excess loop gain: the ADC acts as a gain that changes with the signal's amplitude (its describing function), and the loop, with that gain, must not pass through the −1 point. In practice, this asks for headroom in the gain margin.

They assume that the zero-error band exists: with the reference between two codes, as in ADC quantization, the integrator hunts forever.

Dither

When resolution is short, the firmware can alternate the CCR between two neighboring values over MMM periods. The LC filter takes the average, and the average duty moves in steps of ΔD/M\Delta D/MΔD/M. The price is a component at the frequency fs/Mf_s/Mfs​/M, which must fall well above the crossover and be attenuated by the LC.

/* Dither over M = 4 periods. d4 is the duty in counts with 2 fraction bits
   (d * 2000 * 4). The bit-reversed order spreads the "high" periods. */
static const uint8_t threshold[4] = {0, 2, 1, 3};
static uint32_t k;

uint32_t ccr_with_dither(uint32_t d4)
{
    uint32_t base = d4 >> 2, frac = d4 & 3u;
    return base + (frac > threshold[k++ & 3u]);   /* frac periods out of 4 get +1 */
}

The integrator also needs resolution

With ki q/ΔD≪1k_i\,q/\Delta D \ll 1ki​q/ΔD≪1, each sample moves the integrator by a fraction of a count. If the state is stored in whole counts, the increment rounds to zero and the integrator never moves: an artificial dead band. With float, the H7's FPU (single and double precision, PM0253) keeps the state without this rounding. With integers, store the state with fraction bits, as in Q15 fixed point: that is a design choice, not a requirement of the hardware.
